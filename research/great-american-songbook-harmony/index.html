Great American Songbook Harmony

A small number of recurring harmonic movements explain a surprisingly large share of the standards repertoire. This is the working research behind Sharp Eleven's Drill mode.

Current snapshot: May 2026. This page is a public summary; the internal analysis will keep evolving.

467 charts in the current Great American Songbook analysis corpus
21,275 analyzable adjacent harmonic links after compression
67.14% covered by fifth-cycle chains alone
87.60% covered after adding canonical recurring figures

The Short Version

Jazz standards are not random chord streams. Once repeated versions of the same harmony are compressed, most local motion in this corpus falls into familiar families: fifth-cycle chains, turnarounds, minor cadences, backdoor motion, diminished passing chords, chromatic approaches, and short tonicization patterns.

That is the practical idea behind Drill mode: if you can recognize and play those families fluently in every key, a large part of the Great American Songbook starts to feel like variations on material you already know.

What We Measured

The corpus is a filtered research set derived from a larger Jazz 1460 chart collection. It targets Great American Songbook and closely related Broadway, Hollywood, and Tin Pan Alley standards. It is not a canonical musicological edition; it is a working dataset for building better practice tools.

Before counting, contiguous chords with the same root and simplified quality family are collapsed. For example, Cmaj7 | C6 | Cmaj9 is treated as one major harmonic event. Coverage is then measured on adjacent links between compressed events, not on raw chord symbols, bars, or entire tunes.

The Main Finding

Fifth-cycle motion alone covers about two thirds of the analyzable adjacent links. When we add a small vocabulary of canonical recurring figures, coverage rises to 87.60%. That is the basis for the claim that a handful of progressions accounts for more than 80% of the harmonic motion in this working Songbook corpus.

Common examples include major II-V-I chains, minor II-V-I chains, I-VI-II-V turnarounds, I-VI7-II-V variants, backdoor cadences, II-V oscillations, diminished approaches into II, and temporary moves toward III, IV, or VI.

What This Does Not Mean

The 87.60% number is compressed-link coverage in a working corpus. It does not mean every tune is harmonically identical, that melody and form can be ignored, or that every detected terminal chord is automatically a confirmed local tonic.

The remaining uncovered material is often short, idiomatic, and musically important. The point is not to reduce the repertoire to a formula, but to identify the core movements worth making automatic under the fingers and in the ear.

Where Drill Mode Fits

The current editable drill vocabulary covers 44.05% of the analyzable links as retained drill-core matches. That number is intentionally lower than the research coverage because the drill list is curated for playable practice routines, not for exhaustively labeling every pattern the analyzer can find.

Over time, the research vocabulary will feed more targeted drill families: cadential arrivals, chain restarts, chromatic approaches, common modulation paths, and minor-key variants.
